How many yes or no questions does it take to find one thing? Two questions carry this book: how many for equally likely items, and how few on average when the odds are skewed. One running example, a guessing bot for a menu of 8 dishes, grows a little in every chapter.

Two ideas are the minimum: one answer can at best halve the candidates, and a rare outcome costs more halvings than a common one. The first chapter builds the halving idea with counts. The second carries it over to probabilities and finds the floor no guesser can beat.

The same floor bounds the average length of a prefix code, which is why compression formats are measured against it.

How many yes or no questions does it take to find one dish among 8? This chapter finds the smallest number that always works. It is the yardstick the next chapter measures against.

A friend secretly picked one of 8 dishes, and you may ask only yes or no questions. What is the smallest number of questions that always finds it?

  1. Two well-worded ones

    Two answers have only four outcomes, so they can tell apart at most 4 dishes.

  2. Three, always enough

    Each answer can at best halve the candidates: 8, 4, 2, 1.

  3. Seven, one per dish

    That always works, but each answer then rules out a single dish, not half of them.

A bit is what one yes or no answer tells you when both answers are equally likely.

Think of a coin hidden in one of two cups. One question, "left cup?", settles it, and no answer could settle more, because the answer itself has only two values. That is one bit. With four cups, the first answer leaves two cups and the second leaves one, so four equally likely cups cost two bits. The count grows by one each time the number of cups doubles, which is why bits are counted in powers of two.

People often equate bits with the digits on a screen. The digits are one way to store answers; the bit is the answer itself, and it exists before anything is written down.

Formal

For N equally likely items, the information needed is \log_2 N bits.

Halving search splits the remaining candidates into two equal groups.

With 8 dishes, "first half of the menu?" leaves 4 whatever the answer. The next question leaves 2, and the third leaves 1. An uneven split, say 1 dish against 7, is a gamble: a lucky yes ends the game, but the usual no leaves 7, and the worst case now needs more questions than before. Even splits make the worst case as small as it can be.

The frames show that three is enough. Why can no question do better? An answer has two values, so it splits the candidates into two groups, and in the worst case you land in the larger one, which holds at least half.

Why can no single yes or no question remove more than half of the candidates in the worst case?

  1. Because 8 is a power of two

    The limit holds for any number of candidates; powers of two only make the count come out even.

  2. The worse answer keeps the larger group

    Two answers make two groups, and the larger group holds at least half.

  3. Wording limits what one question can ask

    Wording changes which dishes fall in each group, not the fact that there are only two groups.

When some dishes are ordered far more often than others, can the bot use fewer than three questions on average? This chapter finds the floor for that average. It carries the halving idea from counts to probabilities.

Half of all orders are for one dish and the rest spread over three others. If the bot may change its questions, what happens to the average count?

  1. It stays at three

    Asking about the popular dish first ends half the games after one question.

  2. It drops below three

    Frequent orders get short paths, so the average falls.

  3. It rises above three

    Reordering questions never lengthens the worst path here; it only moves short paths to common dishes.

Halving candidates and halving probability are the same move: an outcome with probability one half needs one question and one with one eighth needs three, as long as each question splits the remaining probability evenly. Probability now plays the part that counts played in .

The bot keeps an order tally: one row per dish, with how many of the week's orders named it. A dish's odds are its count divided by the week's total.

Symbols of this chapter

p(x)
The probability of outcome x, read from the tally as its share of orders; between 0 and 1.
I(x)
The surprisal of x in bits; 1 for p = 1/2, 3 for p = 1/8.
H
The entropy of the whole menu in bits; 1.75 for odds 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/8.

Surprisal is the number of bits an outcome of probability p costs: minus log2 p.

An outcome with probability one half is isolated by one even split, so it costs one bit. One with probability one eighth needs three splits of the probability, so it costs three. Rare outcomes cost more because more halvings stand between them and the rest. A common confusion is to think the cost depends on what the outcome is called rather than how likely it is.

Formal

I(x) = -\log_2 p(x)

Entropy is the average surprisal, and no guesser can beat it on average.

The area reading explains the average. Each bar contributes its probability times its cost, and the bars together cover the whole width of one. For odds one half, one quarter, one eighth and one eighth, the bars give 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.375 + 0.375 = 1.75. No question order can push the area lower, because every outcome already pays exactly its surprisal; any other order makes some common outcome taller.

Formal

H = -\sum_x p(x)\log_2 p(x)

An outcome with probability p costs minus log2 p questions, and the average of those costs, the entropy, is the floor for any guesser. Skewed odds lower the average because common outcomes get short paths.

A guesser may ask "is it A, B or C?" with three possible answers. In the worst case, what share of 12 candidates can one answer remove?

  1. Half of them

    That is the limit for two answers; three answers make three groups.

  2. Two thirds

    Three groups of 4; the worst answer keeps 4 of 12.

  3. All but one

    Only a lucky answer does that; the worst case keeps the largest group.
